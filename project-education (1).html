Project 02 · Education Research · Nuffic · 2025 – present

Evaluating bilingual education programmes

As Lead Researcher at Nuffic, I design and run a large-scale evaluation of bilingual education (TTO) outcomes across more than 20 schools, covering language skills, personal development, and global citizenship, from synthesising existing school frameworks through to questionnaire design, piloting, and statistical validation.

BackgroundThe question

What are the intended and unintended outcomes of bilingual education?

In the Netherlands, 127 schools currently implement bilingual (TTO) education. The TTO framework asks schools to address three core areas, language, personal development, and global citizenship, but each school has room to interpret and implement these in its own way. Before measuring outcomes, I needed to understand what schools were actually aiming for in practice, not just on paper.

SynthesisMaking sense of 62 schools

Finding the shared constructs behind different frameworks

I reviewed policy documents from 62 TTO schools to see which frameworks they referenced for personal development. The results were fragmented: 48% used their own, informally developed approach, 26% referenced the IB Learner Profile, with the remainder split across Global Citizenship, 21st Century Skills, LOB, Cambridge, and UNESCO frameworks.

Rather than picking one framework and ignoring the rest, I mapped where these frameworks actually overlapped conceptually, treating this like a synthesis task: many different sources, in different language, pointing at a smaller set of recurring underlying constructs.

Instrument designFrom constructs to items

A questionnaire built around three pillars, with language as the thread

Rather than treating bilingual education as only a language intervention, I took a pedagogic and ecological view, following Coyle (2018), treating language as a tool that mediates learning, personal development, and global citizenship rather than a separate outcome. Each questionnaire item was written to reflect a personal development or global citizenship construct specifically through its linguistic expression.

Language · critical/reflective, communicative, creative, ethical, confidence
Personal Development · reflective, collaborative, creative, responsible, resilient, balanced
Global Citizenship · self-efficacy, perspective-taking, intercultural communication, global mindedness

Development process

  • Initial orientation and research question formulation
  • Exploratory analysis of school policy documents and construct definitions
  • Methodology design and questionnaire construction, followed by ethical approval
  • Small-scale comprehension testing with a vulnerable target group
  • Larger-scale pilot study to evaluate the questionnaire itself
  • Revision based on pilot feedback, then translation and finalisation

Early comprehension testing with a vulnerable population surfaced real issues: several items were double-barrelled, used overly formal language, or assumed an academic register not appropriate for all tracks (e.g. vmbo). Items were rewritten in plainer, more concrete language, often grounding abstract constructs in specific scenarios (a group project, a classroom discussion) rather than asking about the construct directly.

Pilot analysisTesting the instrument

Checking the questionnaire works before using it at scale

Pilot n = 69. For this public portfolio, the underlying participant-level data has been replaced with a synthetic dataset matching the same analytical structure, confidential research data is not reproduced here. The statistics below reflect that synthetic reproduction of the real analysis workflow.

I ran a pilot study to check whether the three proposed scales, Language, Personal Development, and Global Citizenship, held together statistically before committing to the full questionnaire. I used Cronbach's alpha and item-drop diagnostics to assess internal consistency, and item-level t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction to check for differences between TTO and non-TTO comparison groups, guarding against false positives across 34 separate item-level tests.

All three scales showed strong internal consistency, well above the conventional 0.70 threshold, indicating items within each scale were measuring a coherent underlying construct.

Two items (Q27, Q8) showed a significant raw group difference, both language-related items. After correcting for 34 simultaneous comparisons, neither survived at the conventional 0.05 threshold, an expected and honest result at this pilot sample size, and a reminder not to over-interpret small-sample exploratory comparisons.

What the pilot changed

Item-drop diagnostics confirmed no single item was dragging down scale reliability badly enough to warrant removal, though a few items with comparatively lower item-total correlations were flagged for review before the main study. The correlation structure between items also confirmed the three-pillar grouping largely held together as intended.

ReflectionLimitations

Honest limitations

A pilot sample of 69 has limited power to detect anything but fairly large group differences, so the absence of significant group effects after correction should not be read as evidence of no difference, only that this pilot could not confidently detect one. The main study, now underway across 20+ schools with stratified sampling, is designed to address this directly.
